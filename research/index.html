Research

Programmable nucleic acid assembly

I design interaction rules, screen them across patchy-particle and nucleotide-level models, and carry the surviving designs into experiments. The work spans finite objects, extended arrays, and open three-dimensional lattices.

  3. 03

    Open crystal lattices

    Interaction design for three-dimensional topology

    Positive and negative design are posed as Boolean satisfiability problems and screened with patchy-particle simulations; the surviving designs are realized in DNA origami and verified by scattering and microscopy. One tetrahedron-shaped origami, reprogrammed only at its four sticky ends, assembles selectively into cubic diamond, hexagonal diamond, and ice 0 — precisely the polytype discrimination that makes tetrahedral lattices hard to assemble selectively. Calculations on idealized high-refractive-index analogues of these lattices connect the approach to photonic design.

    Field note

    A hexagonal-diamond connection

    Natural-history context for the three-dimensional lattice work.Read the noteHide the note

    In 1967, a hexagonal polymorph of diamond was described in shock-transformed graphite inclusions from the Canyon Diablo iron meteorite, the body that formed Arizona’s Meteor Crater. Whether those grains are a distinct hexagonal phase or stacking-disordered cubic diamond has been debated, and the interpretation refined, ever since.

    I visited the crater after leading a study that reports selective assembly of the same diamond network at the colloidal scale. The shared problem is polytype selection, not composition: cubic and hexagonal diamond differ only in stacking sequence, which is exactly what makes it hard to show that one has formed rather than the other. The meteoritic case is a long-running example of that difficulty in shock-transformed carbon; the laboratory crystal is assembled from DNA-origami tetrahedra, where the stacking is set by design and read out by scattering.

    Historical context: Frondel & Marvin, Nature 1967, Hanneman et al., Science 1967, and Németh et al., Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A 2023.

Directions

Placing matter on purpose

Programmable self-assembly is a way to place matter deliberately. When the interaction rules between components are designed rather than discovered, a nanoscale building block can be told where to sit relative to its neighbors, and the same block can be reprogrammed to produce a different target. The work above moves from target-specific constructions toward a reusable process: specify a structure, encode it as interaction rules, screen those rules against the structures that compete with them, and build what survives.

Two things follow from precise placement. Components positioned by design — chromophores, proteins, inorganic particles — interact according to where they sit, which is how a designed arrangement becomes a designed function. And the arrangement itself can be transferred: silica preservation turns a soft nucleic acid lattice into a robust inorganic replica, a step toward using these structures as templates for functional metamaterials, where geometry rather than composition sets the optical or transport response. Making that route more general and more predictable is the direction I want to keep working in.
